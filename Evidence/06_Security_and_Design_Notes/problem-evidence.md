# Problem evidence: why a student finance assistant

**Retrieval date for every source below: 2026-10-11.** This page separates (A) evidence from outside sources from (B) numbers that come from Oasis's own SIMULATION. Nothing in sections A and B is user research (the informal conversations are in section C), and no figure is invented: where a figure could not be found or a source could not be opened, the entry says so.

Method and limits: pages were read with a summarising web-fetch tool, so quotes are short extracts as returned by that tool, not byte-exact copies. Some primary pages could not be opened (World Bank report PDF: HTTP 403; FBI IC3 report PDF: connection closed; Indian parliament library: DNS failure). Where only a search-result summary was available, the entry says "summary only" and the confidence is lowered. Confidence: **high** = primary page read; **medium** = reputable page read, or primary figure seen via a summary; **low** = single secondary or summary only.

## B. Oasis's own SIMULATED numbers (not evidence about the real world)

These come from the mock layer (mock fees, mock rates, simulated people) and are labelled MOCK in the product. They show what the tool does in the sandbox, not what a real student saves or what real fraud costs.

- The agent's plan in the scripted demo saves CNY 1,653 against a "typical bank route"; across 8 planning scenarios conversion fees were CNY 4,990 vs CNY 29,378; the 90-day simulation of 5 personas saves CNY 1,542 to 2,963 each. All use mock route spreads and fees: [results.md](results.md).
- 100% of 32 visible and 28 hidden attacks blocked, CNY 0 lost: the same team wrote attacks and defences, with simulated humans ([results.md](results.md)).

## C. What we have not done

**User research.** We held informal conversations with 10 people we know: 6 international students and interns abroad (Vietnam, Japan, Germany, Spain and Tanzania) and 4 parents; notes are in Evidence/interviews.md. It is a small convenience sample, not a survey; finance offices were not interviewed, and we did not survey landlords. What they told us, in brief: 2 of the 6 students met a fake landlord while looking for a home and were not scammed, because the landlord refused a live viewing and offered only pictures, and when someone was sent to check in person the house did not exist; 1 student had trouble opening a bank account abroad, which delayed rent and other payments; 3 of the 4 parents could not send money because the exchange-rate steps were involved, and some did not understand them; 3 interning students found the difference between exchange rates high when spending. These are individual accounts, not measured frequencies. The problem statement above rests on the public sources listed, which are mostly warnings and secondary reports, together with those conversations. A next step would be structured interviews, including finance offices, about how students pay tuition and rent, what they were charged, and whether they met a changed-bank-details message.
